## What it says
I read the PDF text.
- Re-opened 2026-10-14. The fetch tool returned unreadable binary; I extracted the saved PDF's text locally and checked each line below against it. The earlier lines were right; this pass adds the table figures.
- Method: 48 participants (81 percent German university students, mean age 26; 33 women, 15 men) answered work emails in a lab while a "supervisor" interrupted them by telephone or instant message every two minutes, with questions in the same context as the task, in a different context, or none (baseline). Each participant answered 12 emails in each of the three conditions, and the order was counterbalanced.
- Results (abstract): the context of the interruption did not change the cost; people finished interrupted tasks in less time with no difference in quality, and the price was more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. Openness to experience and need for personal structure predicted how costly interruptions were.
- Table 1, task time with the time spent on interruptions taken out: 22.77 minutes with no interruption, 20.31 with same-context interruptions and 20.60 with different-context interruptions (p < .05 for baseline against each). Average errors in the emails did not differ (1.94, 1.93 and 1.84), and interrupted emails were slightly shorter (29.17 and 30.16 words against 31.49).
- Table 3, ratings on a 20-point scale: stress 6.92 with no interruption, 9.46 same context and 9.13 different context (p < .001); frustration 4.73, 6.63 and 6.48 (p < .01); effort 9.50, 11.04 and 11.52. The authors saw significantly higher stress, frustration, workload, effort and pressure after only 20 minutes of interrupted work.
- The authors say interrupted work is done faster but at a price. They also raise the possibility that interruptions do add time, directly after the interruption while the person reorients, and that faster working hides it; they did not measure speed right after an interruption.
- Re-opened 2026-10-14 (the PDF fetched again, text extracted locally): the 48 participants, the two-minute interruption interval, the 20-point scale and Table 3's stress figures (6.92, 9.46, 9.13) hold. The discussion says interrupted people "experienced a higher workload, more stress, higher frustration, more time pressure, and effort", and that "More sophisticated measurements of working speed directly after an interruption must be done to test this".

## Limits
- A lab session of about one and a half hours with student participants and email tasks, a research note of four pages. It measures work with interruptions against work without them, not batching against piecemeal questions, and not software work. Its time measure subtracts the interruptions' own duration, and the finding is a stress cost, not a time cost.
