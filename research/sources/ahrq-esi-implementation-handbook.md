## What it says
- Purpose (p. 1): "The purpose of triage in the emergency department (ED) is to prioritize incoming patients and to identify those who cannot wait to be seen." The level assigned is a proxy for how long a patient can safely wait for a medical screening examination and treatment.
- Why it matters (p. 1): in 2008 there were 123.8 million U.S. emergency department visits and only 18% of patients were seen within 15 minutes. Under-triage leaves a patient at risk of deterioration while waiting. Over-triage spends scarce resources and can take the open bed another patient needs.
- Two questions, not one (p. 2): the scale asks who should be seen first, and for stable patients it also asks what resources the patient will need, so the right patient reaches the right place at the right time. The handbook says the other national five-level scales (Australasia, Canada, United Kingdom) define time targets and ESI does not.
- Five levels (pp. 7 to 14), set by four decision points: A, does the patient need an immediate life-saving intervention; B, is this a patient who should not wait; C, how many resources will the patient need; D, are the vital signs in the danger zone.
  - Level 1: needs an immediate life-saving intervention (airway, breathing, circulation or a sudden change in consciousness); the physician is at the bedside. About 1% to 3% of patients (p. 10).
  - Level 2: should not wait. A high-risk situation, a new confusion or lethargy, or severe pain or distress (a self-rated 7 or more out of 10 may qualify but need not). About 20% to 30% of patients, and 50% to 60% of them are admitted (p. 13).
  - Level 3: stable, predicted to need two or more resources. About 30% to 40% of patients (p. 13).
  - Level 4: predicted to need one resource. Level 5: predicted to need none. Levels 4 and 5 together are about 20% to 35% of volume (pp. 13 to 14).
- The level 2 test (p. 13): the nurse asks "Would I give my last open bed to this patient?" and a yes meets level 2.
- A cap in the method (p. 2): the nurse never estimates beyond two defined resources, so "many" stops at two.
- The bar was revised on evidence (p. 2): a prospective study of 571 level 2 patients at five hospitals found that 20% received an immediate life-saving intervention, so the level 1 criteria were rewritten for version 4.
- Reliability (p. 3): one hospital's comparison of a three-level scale with ESI version 1 found weighted kappa 0.53 for three levels and 0.68 for five. Over 200 nurses at seven hospitals rated 40 written cases with kappa 0.70 to 0.80, and 386 real triage decisions gave weighted kappa 0.69 to 0.87. A pilot of 493 encounters at two hospitals found 77% exact agreement and 22% within one level between a research nurse and a blinded investigator. A trial with 10 nurses and 200 cases found kappa 0.89 for ESI and 0.91 for the Canadian scale.
- Validity (p. 3): the handbook tests whether levels track admission, resource use and 6-month mortality. Levels 1 and 2 used more resources and were admitted more often than levels 4 and 5.
- Adoption (pp. 1 to 2): historically U.S. departments used no standard scale, and before a joint nurse and physician task force reported in 2002 the systems in use were dominated by three-level scales. A 2009 hospital survey reported ESI 57%, three-level 25%, four-level 10%, other five-level 6%, two-level or other 1%, no triage 1%.

## Limits
- A practice handbook by the method's own developers, published through a federal agency. The studies are the developers' and their colleagues'; the handbook summarizes them without method detail beyond sample sizes.
- Emergency department triage is a bedside judgment by an experienced nurse. A ledger of written decisions has no such live clinician, so the transfer to a ledger is an analogy.
- The handbook reports weak spots: in 782 patients over 65, level 1 criteria found only 42% of those who got a life-saving intervention (specificity 99%); pediatric agreement was 0.77 on written cases and 0.57 on live triage (p. 4).
- Dated 2011 (Version 4). The handbook does not say what happens with no triage at all, other than the 18% seen within 15 minutes and the under-triage and over-triage risks.
