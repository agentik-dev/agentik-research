## What it says
- A target article that reviews many experiments on how many items people hold at once. Its argument: Miller's 1956 "seven, plus or minus two" was a rough estimate, and the limit under clean conditions is about four chunks on average, with a range of three to five.
- Method: it collects studies where chunking and rehearsal are blocked, so the count of items is the count of chunks. Four kinds of condition are used: information overload that limits chunks to single items; steps that block recoding into larger chunks; performance discontinuities caused by the limit; and indirect effects of the limit.
- Convergence: from nine studies of immediate recall of spoken words under articulatory suppression, the author derives 17 independent capacity estimates. All but one fall within three to five items, and most fall within three to four. The one outlier is 2.4 items.
- Group and individual spread: group means generally run from about 3 to 5 chunks, while individual subjects' means run more widely, from 2 to 6. In one cited experiment, adults held about 3.5 items when speech was ignored and children held fewer.
- Compound limits: where a person can group items using long-term memory, recall can reach the Miller range of 7 plus or minus 2. The paper calls that a "compound" limit and reserves "pure" for the four-chunk figure.
- Proposed mechanism: the limit belongs to the focus of attention. Other stores that are not capacity-limited exist alongside it.

## Limits
- It measures how many separate items a person holds in mind at once, not how well people use a short written list that they reread. Applying it to a capped ledger is an inference (conjecture). It supports "keep the always-read part short enough to hold", not any figure for a ledger such as 7 or 25.
- The article is a review, written in 2001, with open peer commentary that disputes the number and the method; the four-chunk thesis is contested. The paper itself names seven opposing views.
- I read the Cambridge page for the bibliographic details and the abstract, and the author-hosted full text of the paper for the figures above (the copy's running head shows the year 2000 on its first page and 2001 on later pages).
