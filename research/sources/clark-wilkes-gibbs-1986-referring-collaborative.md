## What it says
- The model: a speaker presents a noun phrase, and the speaker and addressee repair, expand or replace it until both accept it. Quote, abstract: "The preferred procedure is for the speaker to present a simple noun phrase and for the addressee to accept it by allowing the next contribution to begin." They try to minimise their joint effort.
- Design (pages 10-12): eight pairs of Stanford undergraduates (sixteen students: seven men, nine women) played the Tangram matching game of Krauss and Weinheimer. Each pair arranged 12 abstract figures on each of six trials. The director described the figures in a target order across an opaque screen, and the matcher arranged her identical cards to match. The task was repeated six times with the same figures in new orders; six trials took about 25 minutes. The transcripts hold 9792 words for 576 figure placements (12 figures, six trials, eight pairs). The error rate was 2 percent.
- Word counts (page 12, Figure 2): directors used an average of 41 words per figure on trial 1 and 8 words per figure on trial 6 (F(1,35) = 44.31, p < .001). The fall was steepest from trial 1 to trial 2 and had almost disappeared by trial 6. The count covers all the director's words about that figure, not only the first turn.
- Turns (page 12, Figure 3): directors averaged 3.7 speaking turns per figure on trial 1 and about one per figure on trial 6 (F(1,35) = 79.59, p < .001).
- Within a trial (page 13): the director's words fell by 4.6 words per position from position 1 to position 12 (F(1,77) = 40.01, p < .001) on trial 1, and the within-trial decline was smaller on trials 2 and 6 (1.0 and 0.4 words per position).
- Figure difficulty (page 16): the hardest figure averaged 26.5 words per trial and drew 39.6 words on trial 1; the easiest averaged 9.7 words per trial and drew 24 on trial 1.
- Basic exchanges (page 17): the percentage of basic exchanges, where the matcher accepts the director's first presentation without reworking it, rose over trials 1 to 6 as 18, 55, 75, 80, 88 and 84 percent (F(1,55) = 84.19, p < .001). The authors say this accounts for much of the drop in words and turns.
- The example for one figure across trials 1 to 6 (page 12): on trial 1 the director described it at length as a person ice skating with two arms stuck out in front; by trial 6 the whole reference was "The ice skater." From trial 2 on, directors referred to the figure with a definite description rather than describing it.
- On the earlier finding (page 10): the authors report that Krauss and Weinheimer (1964) found that people referring repeatedly to the same thing tended to shorten their noun phrases, but only when their listeners could speak in return. That is the authors' account of the 1964 paper, not my reading of it.

## Limits
- One laboratory task with abstract shapes, 16 students, and partners who stay the same across trials. The paper does not test a new partner or an outsider; for the outsider result see schober-clark-1989-addressees-overhearers.
- The figure for trial 6 is a mean of 8 words, not one or two. The older synthesis and notes describe the shrinkage as from about 40 words to one or two, taken from the ice-skater example on Wikipedia; the paper's own mean at trial 6 is 8 words per figure.
- I read pages 1-4 and 9-18 of 39 as page images because the file is a scan with no text layer. Values for trials 2 to 5 appear only in Figure 2 and I did not transcribe them.
- Krauss and Weinheimer 1964 (Psychonomic Science) could not be opened: the publisher page redirected to a login, and no free copy turned up. It is cited here only as these authors describe it.
