## What it says
- Two randomized online studies (Study 1: 2,288 respondents; Study 2: 2,211 respondents; a German general-population sample with quotas for age, gender and education) varied how plain language summaries of psychological meta-analyses were written. Outcomes were perceived accessibility, understanding and empowerment (1 to 8 scales) and content knowledge (forced-choice items).
- Replacing technical terms with plain wording improved content knowledge: 68.84 percent correct, against 63.60 percent with no explanation. A glossary did not differ significantly from no explanation on knowledge.
- Replacing or glossarizing technical terms improved user experience over no explanation (replacement against none, accessibility b = 0.545, p < 0.001).
- Glossaries of statistical terms lowered user experience in most comparisons (for example, glossary against a qualitative statement, accessibility b = -0.583, p < 0.001, for low-complexity summaries). For high-complexity summaries, a glossary plus a qualitative statement lowered content knowledge (61.82 percent against 68.55 to 69.53 percent in other conditions).
- Structuring the text improved accessibility for high-complexity summaries (b = 0.197, p = 0.044) but not for low-complexity ones.
- Adding information on how the synthesized studies operationalized their questions lowered accessibility (b = -0.338, p = 0.001) with no significant gain in knowledge.

## Limits
- Lay readers of psychology summaries, not engineers reading agent reports; the German sample is not representative; only eight summaries were tested.
- The page is long and I read about the first 100,000 characters, so the general discussion and some limits were not read.
- It is the nearest measured test I found of "replace the jargon" against "add a legend".
