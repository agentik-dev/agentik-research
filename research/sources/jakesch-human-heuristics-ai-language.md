## What it says
- Six experiments (three main, three validation) with 4,600 participants who read 7,600 self-presentations in professional, hospitality and dating contexts. Participants could not reliably tell human text from AI text: accuracy in the main experiments was 50 to 52 percent (hospitality 52.2, dating with monetary incentive 51.6, professional with feedback 51.2).
- Readers used heuristics that misled them. They took grammatical problems, long words and rare word pairs as signs of AI, though these were more common in human text; and they took first-person pronouns, contractions and family words as signs of a human, though these appeared equally in both.
- Text optimized to look human was judged human 65.7 percent of the time, against 51.6 percent for regular AI text and 51.7 percent for human text. Statistical classifiers did better than people (57.6 to 58.8 percent).
- Participants' stated reasons were content (40 percent of responses), grammar (28 percent) and tone (24 percent).

## Limits
- Short personal self-presentations written by 2022-era models. It measures detection by lay readers, not whether a detected machine tone lowers trust, and not detection of technical prose.
