## What it says
Section names as the paper prints them; I read the PDF text.
- Introduction ("Empirical Cost of Interruption"), citing earlier studies: interrupting at random moments can cause users to take up to 30 percent longer to resume, commit up to twice the errors and feel up to twice the negative affect, compared with interrupting at subtask boundaries. The paper adds that interrupting at boundaries with lower mental workload costs meaningfully less than at boundaries with higher workload.
- Its own experiment 1: 12 users did desktop tasks (video editing, route planning, document editing) and were interrupted at chosen subtask boundaries by a peripheral task; 360 resumption-lag values were collected (337 after removing about 6 percent as outliers or errors). Resumption lag fell into three classes of cost (low, medium and high), with the paper noting that a system may only be able to separate three classes.
- A model built on task structure (the level of the boundary, mental carry-over, difficulty of the next subtask) classified cost correctly in 63.2 percent of cases in cross-validation, and an experiment 2 with 12 other users and different tasks tested it again. The error of calling a high-cost boundary low-cost was 4.7 percent.

## Limits
- The 30 percent and twice-the-errors figures are the authors' summary of prior work, not their own result. Tasks are desktop tasks done by 12 young adults, not software work, and the interruptions were small peripheral tasks. The paper measures when to interrupt, not whether several questions asked together cost less than the same questions asked one at a time.
