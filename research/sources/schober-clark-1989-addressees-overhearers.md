## What it says
- The claim (abstract): speakers design utterances for the common ground they share with their addressees, and addressees can also collaborate with the speaker to reach the mutual belief that they understood. Quote: "As predicted, addressees were more accurate at arranging the figures than overhearers even when the overhearers heard every word."
- Design, Experiment 1 (pages 214-216): ten pairs of students who did not know each other did the Tangram matching task of Krauss and Weinheimer: a director described 12 figures in order and a matcher placed cards, over six trials. Forty other students then listened to tape recordings of those conversations and did the matcher's task as overhearers. Half the overhearers heard from trial 1 (early) and half only from trial 3 (late); within each group half could pause the tape and half could not. Each recording was heard by four overhearers.
- Shortening reproduced (page 217, Figure 2): directors began at about 73 words per figure and ended at about 13; matchers began at about 39 and ended at about 3. The director's turns per figure fell from 7.8 to 1.1, and time per figure from about 39 seconds on trial 1 to about 6 seconds on trial 6. The authors say this corroborates Krauss and Weinheimer's classic observations on repeated reference.
- Accuracy over all trials (page 218): early overhearers 88 percent correct, late overhearers 68 percent (F(1,38) = 20.26, p < .001). Matchers 99 percent against early overhearers 88 percent (F(1,28) = 10.51, p < .005), although the early overhearers heard every word.
- By trial (page 219, Figure 3): matchers averaged 93 percent on trial 1 and were perfect from trial 4. Early overhearers began at 81 percent and reached 95 percent by trial 6. Late overhearers began at 55 percent and reached 73 percent.
- Pausing did not help (page 219): 89 and 88 percent for early overhearers with and without a pause button, and 73 and 68 percent for late overhearers; the differences were not significant. Most overhearers believed pausing had helped.
- Overhearers placed only 63 percent of their cards by the speakers' completion points, against 99 percent for matchers (page 219).
- Page 217 also records that speakers' final names for one figure varied widely between pairs: "the rice bag", "the whale" and "the baby in a straitjacket".

## Limits
- I read pages 1 to 9 of 22 as page images (a scan with no text layer): the abstract, the predictions, the method and the Experiment 1 results. I did not read the later experiments, so I make no claim about them.
- Students listening to recordings, not live eavesdroppers, on a picture-matching task. Overhearers who started late had also missed the negotiation that built each name.
- The data are accuracy of placement on a game, not comprehension of working prose. The step from this to a human reading agent shorthand is an analogy; see garrod-2007-graphical-symbol-systems for a related finding on drawings.
