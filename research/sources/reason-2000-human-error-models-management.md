## What it says
- The article contrasts a person approach (blame the individual who made the unsafe act) with a system approach (assume people err, and build defences). Its section "The Swiss cheese model of system accidents" is the source of the model.
- The model in two lines. Defences, barriers and safeguards stand between hazards and harm, in layers made of engineered systems, people and procedures. Each layer has holes that open, shut and move, and an accident needs the holes in many layers to line up at the same moment.
- Quote, section "The Swiss cheese model of system accidents": "In reality, however, they are more like slices of Swiss cheese, having many holes". The sentence it follows says that in an ideal world each defensive layer would be intact.
- The holes come from two sources. Active failures are unsafe acts by people in direct contact with the system (slips, lapses, mistakes, procedural violations), and their effects are short-lived. Latent conditions are the "resident pathogens" built in by designers, builders, procedure writers and managers, which can lie dormant for years and can be found and fixed before an accident.
- The author's remedy follows from that split: build better defences and drain the latent conditions, because active failures keep coming (the article likens them to mosquitoes that can be swatted one at a time). Figure caption: the model shows how "defences, barriers, and safeguards may be penetrated by an accident trajectory."

## Limits
- A conceptual article of three pages with no measurement. It models accidents in medicine and other high-hazard industries; it does not test the model.
- I read the full text on PubMed Central through WebFetch. The summary tool returned the wording; I confirmed the phrases "momentarily line up" and "slices of Swiss cheese" in a second fetch. The caption is quoted from the first fetch only.
- The carry-over to unattended agent runs (a lock file that stopped a run is one layer holding) is our analogy, not a claim of the article.
