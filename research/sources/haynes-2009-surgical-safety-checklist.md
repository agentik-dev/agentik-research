## What it says
- Design: a prospective before-and-after study at eight hospitals in eight cities (Toronto, New Delhi, Amman, Auckland, Manila, Ifakara, London, Seattle), October 2007 to September 2008, inside the World Health Organization's Safe Surgery Saves Lives program. Sites were chosen for spread across regions and income levels.
- Patients: 3733 consecutively enrolled patients aged 16 or older having noncardiac surgery in the baseline period, then 3955 after the checklist was introduced, from the same operating rooms. The primary end point was complications, including death, in hospital within 30 days of the operation.
- Intervention: a 19-item checklist read aloud by the team at three points: before anaesthesia (sign in), before skin incision (time out) and before the patient leaves the operating room (sign out). The items include confirming identity and site, antibiotic timing, and instrument and sponge counts. Sites adopted it within 1 week to 1 month.
- Result, death in hospital: 1.5 percent before, 0.8 percent after (P = 0.003).
- Result, any inpatient complication: 11.0 percent before, 7.0 percent after (P < 0.001). Surgical-site infection fell from 6.2 to 3.4 percent (P < 0.001, Table 5); unplanned return to the operating room fell from 2.4 to 1.8 percent (P = 0.047); pneumonia did not change significantly (1.1 to 1.3 percent, P = 0.46).
- By income: complications fell from 10.3 to 7.1 percent at high-income sites and from 11.7 to 6.8 percent at lower-income sites (both P < 0.001). Death fell from 0.9 to 0.6 percent at high-income sites (P = 0.18, not significant) and from 2.1 to 1.0 percent at lower-income sites (P = 0.006).
- The authors state that the reduction was not owed to one site: the result held when any one site was removed (P < 0.05).
- Quote, abstract, Conclusions: the checklist "was associated with concomitant reductions in the rates of death and complications" in a diverse group of hospitals.

## Limits
- Not randomised. The authors say randomising by operating room risked cross-contamination, so they compared pre-intervention data with post-intervention data, which leaves confounding by secular trends. They name that danger and the Hawthorne effect, and argue that a trend is unlikely because the study ran for under 1 year, a United States surgical-quality cohort showed no substantial change in 2007, rates of urgent cases and outpatient surgery did not change, and adjusting for case mix did not alter the result.
- Only inpatient complications were counted, so outpatient surgery is under-counted. Adherence to the briefings and debriefings could not be measured. The authors say further study is needed on mechanism and durability.
- The paper says it was supported by grants from the World Health Organization, whose program the study belongs to, and reports no conflict of interest relevant to the article.
- I read the full text through a copy in the Harvard open repository (the page the url names) and the PubMed abstract record through Europe PMC; the publisher's own page returned HTTP 403. The Patient Safety Network summary page I also opened gives only the eight hospitals, the 19 items and the words "significant reductions in mortality and inpatient complications", with no figures.
- A checklist read by humans in an operating room is an analogy for a deterministic gate on an unattended run, not evidence for it.
