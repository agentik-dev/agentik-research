## What it says
- Each participant read two inverted pyramid news stories (main point first) and two chronological narrative stories. The researchers measured secondary-task reaction times, meaning how fast participants answered beeps while reading and afterwards.
- Men responded faster to the inverted pyramid stories and slower to the narrative ones. The researchers say this could mean more engagement with narrative or simply easier processing of the inverted pyramid.
- Women were slower than men overall but similar across the two styles. The authors conclude that story structure does not explain the drop in female readership.
- The Missouri School of Journalism release I also opened (journalism.missouri.edu, 2011-04-12) gives the same design and results.

## Limits
- I read two news summaries, not the paper. Neither reports the number of participants. The year differs between them (2010 against 2011), so confirm against the journal before citing.
- News stories and a reaction-time proxy for effort, not comprehension or decisions on a report. A search snippet attributes a marginal comprehension advantage for the inverted pyramid to the first author's dissertation; the record returned HTTP 403, so I do not rely on it.
- It is one of the few direct tests of conclusion-first order against chronological order that I found, and its result is narrow.
