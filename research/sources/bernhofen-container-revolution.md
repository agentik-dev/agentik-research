## What it says
- Sources opened: the working paper PDF above (full text, read for every figure below), the Lund University research portal record and the Brighton research portal record for the journal version (bibliographic details and abstract only; the publisher's page returned HTTP 403).
- Method: a panel of product-level bilateral trade flows for 157 countries, 1962-1990. The treatment is the year a pair of countries first both processed cargo through container port or rail facilities. Products are split by whether a 1968 German engineers' study judged them suitable for containers. The sample holds 4 years before container shipping, the 1966-1983 adoption period, and 7 years after the last adoption. Of 157 countries, 122 started container handling in 1966-1983 and 35 had not by 1990.
- Headline for trade among rich countries: the working paper's abstract puts the cumulative average effect over 20 years at "about 700%". The body gives the benchmark estimates: +320% in the adoption year, then +232%, +156% and +82% over the next three five-year periods, summing to 790%. The authors report that a pre-treatment test is insignificant, so they read the effect as causal.
- Comparison with policy: a free trade agreement raised trade 26% at once and 45% cumulatively. Joint GATT membership raised it 92% at once and 285% cumulatively, less than half the container effect. The authors also find the effect on goods that cannot be containerised was not statistically different from the effect on goods that can.
- Standards: the paper describes a standards sequence before adoption. In 1961 a US maritime board fixed the nominal dimensions (8 feet wide, 8 feet high, 10, 20, 30 and 40 feet long). The International Organization for Standardization adopted the 10-, 20-, 30- and 40-foot sizes in 1964, and strength and lifting standards in 1965. Container leasing firms then had reason to expand, and the paper says the ability of land and sea carriers to handle each other's containers set the foundation for global adoption.
- Context: world trade grew in real terms from 0.45 trillion dollars in the early 1960s to 3.4 trillion in 1990 (a factor of about 7). The authors quote a 2006 historian who held that how much the container matters is "impossible to quantify", and say their paper fills that gap.
- Journal abstract (Lund and Brighton records, paraphrased): finds "economically large concurrent and cumulative effects" of containerisation; the records give no percentage.

## Limits
- The measured effect is of container technology and its intermodal system as a whole, not of the size standard alone. The standard's role is described in the narrative as a precondition, with no separate estimate (a mechanism claim, not a measured one).
- The 700% and 790% figures come from the working paper. The journal abstract does not state them and describes a method that uses product-level containerisability and container-use intensity. The journal text may give other numbers. Cite the working paper by name until the journal text is read.
- The "about 700%" in the abstract and the 790% in the body differ; the paper does not reconcile them in the pages I read. The effects are for North-North trade only; the paper reports smaller effects for other country pairs.
- An econometric estimate from 1962-1990 trade data, with the usual identification limits.
