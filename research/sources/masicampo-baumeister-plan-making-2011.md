## What it says
I opened the author-hosted Online First PDF (the fetch tool returned unreadable binary, so I extracted its text locally) and the Europe PMC record of the abstract (PMID 21688924).
- Claim: an unfinished goal keeps generating thoughts and interference even though no work has been done; a specific plan for the goal can switch that activity off before the goal is met. In the authors' words: "Once a plan is made, the drive to attain a goal is suspended--allowing goal-related cognitive activity to cease--and is resumed at the specified later time." (abstract)
- Study 1 method: 73 undergraduates (69 analyzed after four guessed the link between the two parts). Each was randomly assigned to one of three conditions. In the unfulfilled condition they wrote about two important tasks due in the next few days, with no when, where or how yet. In the plan condition they wrote about the same kind of tasks and then said in detail how, when and where they would do them. In the control condition they wrote about two tasks they had just finished. All then read about 3,200 words of a novel and rated how far thoughts of the tasks distracted them (1 to 7).
- Study 1 result: distraction by task thoughts averaged 3.00 (unfulfilled), 1.82 (control) and 1.77 (plan); plan against unfulfilled F(1, 66) = 6.41, p = .014, and plan against control showed no difference (p = .93). Mind wandering at least once: 65.2 percent in the unfulfilled group against 33.3 percent in the plan group (p = .043). Reading comprehension out of eight: 6.13 unfulfilled, 6.93 control, 6.94 plan.
- Study 4 method: 97 undergraduates in goal, plan or control conditions. The goal and plan groups learned of a later brainstorming task; the plan group also committed to a when-then plan for it. A five-minute anagram task came first.
- Study 4 result: anagrams solved averaged 6.55 (goal), 8.39 (control) and 9.55 (plan); plan against goal p = .012, control against plan no difference. 70 percent of the plan group later used the suggested strategy, against 13 percent of the goal group and 6 percent of the control group. The anagram benefit followed the plans that people went on to carry out.
- Abstract, other studies: goal-related words stayed more accessible in memory until a plan was made (Studies 2 and 3); changes in goal-related emotion did not appear necessary (Studies 5A and 5B).

## Limits
- Undergraduate lab samples of 69 and 97 people; short tasks, mostly errands and one exam goal; intrusion measured by self-report in Study 1. The plans were written how-when-where statements, not a hand-off document for another worker, and the paper is about a person's own attention, not a model's context.
- I found no direct replication of the plan-making studies in the searches I ran. The related Zeigarnik memory effect is itself contested; see ghibellini-meier-zeigarnik-meta-2025.md.
- The same authors' 2012 paper on plans closing the mind to alternatives appeared in a search result only; I did not open it.
